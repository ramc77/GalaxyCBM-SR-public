\title{\bfseries Interpretable Machine Learning for Galaxy Morphology:
A Calibrated Symbolic Concept Bottleneck, and Why Its Explanations Do Not
Transfer Across Surveys}

\author{
  Ram Chand\thanks{Correspondence: \texttt{ram.chand2k11@yahoo.com}}\\[2pt]
  \normalsize Department of Natural Sciences,\\
  \normalsize The Begum Nusrat Bhutto Women University, Sukkur, Sindh, Pakistan
}

\date{\today}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Deep networks classify galaxy morphology at survey scale but expose no
auditable reasoning, and post-hoc attributions explain a surrogate rather than
the deployed decision. We present the first Concept Bottleneck Model for
galaxy morphology: a \textsc{Zoobot} ConvNeXt predicts seventeen physically
named concepts---ten Galaxy Zoo tasks and seven \textsc{statmorph}
statistics---and a symbolic-regression head of 153 operator nodes maps them to
a Hubble type under split-conformal uncertainty. The seven governing equations
are printed in full, so the deployed decision can be read and criticised
directly. On $40\,498$ Galaxy Zoo Evo galaxies we quantify what
interpretability costs rather than claiming it is free: an unconstrained
ConvNeXt reaches $\kappa=0.601$ against the symbolic head's $0.571$, while
symbolic, dense-linear and boosted-tree heads over the same concepts agree to
within $0.002$. The cost is therefore attributable to the concept layer, not to
the symbolic form, which replaces $1.5\times10^{7}$ parameters at no further
penalty. Two findings qualify this. Conformal coverage is marginally exact
($88.9\%$ against $90\%$) but conditionally severe: per-class coverage spans
$0.98$ for ellipticals to $0.08$ for the rarest spiral, so the guarantee is met
by over-covering the dominant class. Class-conditional calibration lifts
worst-case coverage to $0.885$ but triples mean set size and removes singleton
predictions altogether---the reliability statement is repaired, the information
is not. Applying the frozen pipeline to $161\,395$ Euclid Q1 galaxies preserves
accuracy while a symbolic refit recovers only $42\%$ of the reference rules'
concept usage. Accuracy transfer and interpretation transfer are distinct
properties, and an accuracy-only audit certifies a pipeline whose explanations
have silently changed. Code, seeds and a reproducible benchmark for concept
fidelity, conditional coverage and rule stability are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Galaxy morphology encodes assembly history. Mergers, secular disc growth,
bar-driven inflows and quenching each leave structural signatures, and the
correlation of those signatures with mass, environment and star-formation rate
underpins much of our picture of galaxy evolution
\citep{hubble1926,conselice2014}. Surveys now deliver morphological data at a
volume that forecloses visual classification: Galaxy Zoo has aggregated
$\sim\!10^{8}$ volunteer classifications, and Euclid, LSST and Roman will
image billions of resolved sources.

Automated classifiers meet this volume, but the dominant approach---end-to-end
convolutional networks trained directly to a morphological
target~\citep{dieleman2015,dominguez2018,walmsley2022}---produces decisions in
a representation with no physical referent. When such a model assigns a galaxy
to Sb, nothing in its output identifies which structural property drove the
assignment. The standard remedy, post-hoc attribution via SHAP or saliency
maps~\citep{lundberg2017,smilkov2017}, fits a second model to approximate the
first; \citet{rudin2019} argues that this approximation routinely disagrees
with the computation it purports to describe, and that in consequential
settings one should instead constrain the model to be interpretable by
construction.

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)~\citep{koh2020} implement that constraint. Every
prediction is forced through an intermediate layer of individually supervised,
named concepts; the final classifier sees nothing else. The architecture
remains active in interpretable-ML research, with post-hoc
construction~\citep{yuksekgonul2023} and label-free concept
discovery~\citep{oikarinen2023} both directed at the same practical
obstacle: obtaining a concept vocabulary and the annotations to supervise it.
Galaxy morphology does not have that problem. The Galaxy Zoo decision tree
already enumerates a curated perceptual
vocabulary (bar strength, bulge prominence, spiral arm count and winding,
edge-on orientation, disturbance), and non-parametric morphology supplies a
quantitative one (concentration, asymmetry, smoothness, Gini, $M_{20}$,
Sérsic index, effective radius) with three decades of physical calibration
behind it~\citep{conselice2003,lotz2004,rodriguez2019}. These are the
quantities in which morphologists already reason. Despite this, no published
CBM exists for galaxy morphology.

This paper closes that gap and adds three components that we argue are
prerequisites for deploying an interpretable classifier on survey data.

\emph{A symbolic decision head.} We replace the CBM's usual dense linear
layer with compact analytic expressions discovered by symbolic
regression~\citep{cranmer2023}. The deployed classifier is then a table of
seven equations that an astronomer can read, not a weight matrix over concepts.
Symbolic regression has been applied to astronomical classification boundaries,
notably in star--galaxy--quasar
separation~\citep{deshpande2026,llorella2025}, where compact analytic
functions of photometric colours recover competitive accuracy. Here the
symbolic model operates one level higher: its inputs are not raw observables
but predicted physical concepts, and the expression it recovers constitutes
the classifier itself rather than a summary of one.

\emph{Calibrated set-valued prediction.} Interpretability does not confer
reliability: a legible rule can be confidently wrong. We wrap the symbolic
head in split-conformal prediction~\citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2023}, which
converts point predictions into label sets whose marginal coverage is
guaranteed without assuming the underlying score is a calibrated probability.
We then measure \emph{conditional} coverage per class, and find that the
marginal guarantee is nearly vacuous for the rarest morphologies
(Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\emph{An interpretability audit under domain shift.} Instruments differ in
pixel scale, passband and point-spread function, and non-parametric morphology
statistics are known to respond to all three~\citep{ferrari2015}. We therefore
ask a question that accuracy-based robustness studies cannot answer: after
transfer to a new survey, does a symbolic refit recover the same rules? We
quantify this with a concept-usage Jaccard index between reference and refit
rule sets. On Euclid Q1 the answer is no, even though accuracy is preserved
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The first Concept Bottleneck Model for galaxy morphology, jointly
supervised on Galaxy Zoo vote fractions and \textsc{statmorph} structural
statistics, with per-concept fidelity reported against explicit trivial
baselines (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}).
\item A symbolic concept head---153 operator nodes across seven classes,
printed in full---together with a measurement of what it costs: the concept
bottleneck gives up $0.030$ in $\kappa$ against an unconstrained ConvNeXt,
while the symbolic head matches a dense linear head and gradient-boosted trees
over the same concepts to within $0.002$. Expressing the classifier
symbolically is therefore free; restricting it to named concepts is not
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).
\item Evidence that marginal conformal coverage is an inadequate reliability
statement under the class imbalance endemic to morphology catalogues, with
per-class coverage spanning an order of magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}).
\item A rule-stability metric and the finding that symbolic explanations do
not transfer across surveys even when accuracy does, establishing
interpretation transfer as a target for evaluation distinct from accuracy
transfer (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}).
\end{enumerate}

Code, configurations, random seeds, trained rules and every number quoted
below are released (Section~\ref{sec:availability}).

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Primary sample}

We use Galaxy Zoo Evo~\citep{walmsley2025gzevo}, restricted to the DESI Legacy
Imaging pool (\code{dataset\_name = gz\_desi}), which aggregates the GZD-5 and
GZD-8 campaigns~\citep{walmsley2022,walmsley2023desi}. We process twelve of
the thirty released training shards, $265\,692$ raw rows, delivered as
$224\times224$ three-band cutouts at the DECaLS native pixel scale of
$0.262''$. This is a deliberate subsample: \citet{walmsley2024scaling} show
that morphology-model performance on Galaxy Zoo imagery continues to improve
with training-set size, so the absolute metrics reported here should be read
as a floor rather than a ceiling for the architecture.

\subsection{Structural concepts}

We compute concentration $C$, asymmetry $A$, smoothness $S$, the Gini
coefficient, $M_{20}$, Sérsic index $n$ and elliptical half-light radius
$r_{\rm eff}$ for every cutout with \textsc{statmorph}
v0.7.2~\citep{rodriguez2019}. Segmentation uses \code{photutils}
\code{detect\_sources} at a $2.5\sigma$ threshold above a background estimated
from the frame corners; because Galaxy Zoo cutouts are centred and tightly
cropped, a global median background is biased high by the source itself, and
the corner estimate removes that bias. The same estimate is subtracted before
the morphology call.

Failures are recorded rather than silently propagated. A per-object bitmask
flags \textsc{statmorph} internal warnings, non-convergent Sérsic fits, absent
segmentation, and a $\SI{60}{\second}$ per-object wall-clock timeout---a small
population of images drives the Sérsic optimiser into non-terminating
iteration. Of $265\,692$ processed objects, $127\,816$ ($48.1\%$) carry at
least one flag and are excluded before label construction. A downstream
assertion refuses to proceed if any concept is \code{NaN} without a
corresponding flag, so no silent failure can reach the model.

\subsection{Morphological labels}

Hubble types $\{$E, S0, Sa, Sb, Sc, Sd, Irr$\}$ are derived from GZD-5 vote
fractions through a compact traversal of the Galaxy Zoo decision tree:
smooth-or-featured $\rightarrow$ merger test $\rightarrow$ edge-on test
$\rightarrow$ spiral-arm test $\rightarrow$ winding $\times$ bulge size for
spiral subtype. At each node we take the plurality answer subject to a
clean-sample threshold of $0.5$; objects for which no answer clears threshold
at any node are discarded as unclassified.

The full selection is therefore: $265\,692$ objects processed, $137\,876$
surviving the quality cuts of the previous subsection, and $40\,498$
retained after label derivation. The $97\,378$ objects lost at the final
step ($70.6\%$ of those entering it) failed the $0.5$ threshold somewhere in
the tree, overwhelmingly at the spiral sub-type nodes where volunteers
disagree most. That fraction is a property of the threshold, not of the
imaging: a lower threshold retains more galaxies at the cost of noisier
labels, and the value adopted here follows the clean-sample convention of
\citet{walmsley2022}. The surviving sample is thus biased toward
morphologically unambiguous systems, which inflates absolute accuracy
relative to a magnitude-limited sample and is the reason we compare methods
on identical splits rather than quoting accuracy against the literature.

\subsection{Splits and leakage control}

We partition into train ($24\,299$), validation ($6\,075$), calibration
($4\,049$) and test ($6\,075$) subsets, stratified by Hubble type under a fixed
seed. Leakage is excluded on two axes: exact object-identifier collision, and
on-sky proximity, rejecting any cross-split pair separated by less than
$3''$ (\code{astropy} \code{SkyCoord} cross-match). The second test matters
because independent vote aggregation can produce distinct identifiers for the
same physical object.

The resulting class distribution is severely imbalanced
(Table~\ref{tab:balance}): ellipticals constitute $66.7\%$ of the sample while
Sa and Sd together account for $0.6\%$. This imbalance is intrinsic to
volunteer-classified magnitude-limited samples, not an artefact of our cuts,
and it governs the interpretation of every aggregate metric below.

\label{sec:methods}

The pipeline comprises three stages, each frozen before the next is evaluated.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} states the procedure in full; the subsections
below give the reasoning behind each design choice. Table~\ref{tab:glossary}
collects the machine-learning terms used repeatedly in what follows, each also
defined at first use in the text.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\SetAlgoLined
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Images $\{x_i\}$; concept targets $\{c_i\}$ from Galaxy Zoo votes and
\textsc{statmorph}; Hubble labels $\{y_i\}$; splits
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr},\mathcal{D}_{\rm val},\mathcal{D}_{\rm cal},
\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$; miscoverage level $\alpha$}
\KwOut{Rule set $\{g_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$; conformal threshold $\hat q$}
\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Stage 1 --- concept predictor}\;
Initialise backbone $f_\theta$ from \textsc{Zoobot} pretrained weights\;
Attach one linear head per concept: softmax for the $M$ categorical Galaxy
Zoo tasks, scalar for the $P$ continuous structural statistics\;
Minimise
$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_m\,\mathrm{CE}(\hat c_m, c_m)
             + \sum_{p=1}^{P}\lambda_p\,\mathrm{SmoothL1}(\hat c_p, c_p)$
on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, early-stopping on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm val}$\;
Missing categorical targets are masked via \code{ignore\_index}; missing
continuous targets are masked in the loss\;
Emit predicted concept vectors $\hat c_i$ for \emph{all} splits\;
\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Stage 2 --- symbolic head} \tcp*{consumes $\hat c$, never $c$}
\For{each class $k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$}{
  Form binary target $z_i = \mathbb{1}[y_i = k]$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$\;
  Run symbolic regression on $(\hat c, z)$ under a complexity budget,
  obtaining a Pareto front $\mathcal{P}_k$ of (expression, complexity) pairs\;
  \For{each candidate $g \in \mathcal{P}_k$}{
    Score $g$ by $R$-fold stratified cross-validated accuracy on held-out
    folds of $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$\;
  }
  $g_k \leftarrow$ candidate maximising CV accuracy, ties broken toward
  lower complexity\;
}
Predict $\hat y(\hat c) = \arg\max_k g_k(\hat c)$\;
\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Stage 3 --- conformal calibration}\;
$s_i \leftarrow 1 - \mathrm{softmax}_k\!\left[g_k(\hat c_i)\right]_{y_i}$
for $i \in \mathcal{D}_{\rm cal}$ \tcp*{least-ambiguous-set score}
$\hat q \leftarrow \lceil (n_{\rm cal}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil / n_{\rm cal}$
empirical quantile of $\{s_i\}$\;
$C(x) \leftarrow \{k : 1 - \mathrm{softmax}_k[g(\hat c)] \le \hat q\}$\;
\Return $\{g_k\},\ \hat q$\;
\caption{Symbolic concept-bottleneck classification with conformal
uncertainty.}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Stage 1: concept predictor}
\label{sec:stage1}

\paragraph{The concept vocabulary.}
The concept layer is the interface between the image and the symbolic
classifier, and it is the only channel through which pixels can influence the
predicted Hubble type. We therefore state it in full before describing how it
is trained. Table~\ref{tab:concepts} lists all seventeen concepts, their
provenance, and the physical property each is intended to carry.

Ten are \emph{perceptual}: the Galaxy Zoo decision-tree tasks, each a question
put to volunteers about a visible structure (is the galaxy smooth or featured;
is the disc edge-on; is there a bar; how tightly are the arms wound). Their
targets are the volunteer vote fractions over the permitted answers, so a
categorical concept with $J$ answers is supervised as a $J$-way distribution
rather than a single label. Seven are \emph{structural}: the non-parametric
morphology statistics computed by \textsc{statmorph}
(Section~\ref{sec:data}), each with an established physical reading
--- concentration tracks the bulge-to-total light ratio, asymmetry and
smoothness track merger-driven and star-formation-driven irregularity, Gini
and $M_{20}$ track how the light is distributed and where the brightest
regions sit, and the S\'ersic index and effective radius parametrise the
overall light profile \citep{conselice2003,lotz2004,rodriguez2019}.

One bookkeeping point matters for reading the rules in
Appendix~\ref{app:rules}. The symbolic head does not consume seventeen
numbers. Each categorical concept enters as one predicted vote fraction per
permitted answer, so the ten perceptual concepts expand to thirty-two
answer-level quantities --- for the two binary tasks one of the two
complementary fractions is dropped, since it carries no independent
information --- which with the seven structural statistics gives thirty-nine
inputs. We use \emph{concept} for the seventeen physical
quantities and \emph{concept variable} for the thirty-nine numbers a rule may
reference. Concept fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}) is reported per
concept; rule composition (Section~\ref{sec:rules}) is reported per concept
variable.

\paragraph{Training.}
We fine-tune a \textsc{Zoobot} \code{convnext\_nano}
backbone~\citep{walmsley2023zoobot,liu2022convnext}, pretrained on Galaxy Zoo
imaging, attaching one linear head per concept: cross-entropy for the ten
categorical Galaxy Zoo tasks, smooth-$L_1$ for the seven continuous structural
statistics, summed with equal weight across provenance classes. Optimisation
uses AdamW at learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, mixed
precision, gradient clipping at $1.0$, and early stopping on validation loss
within a 40-epoch budget.

Concept fidelity is reported per concept against an explicit trivial baseline:
area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) against $0.5$ for
categorical concepts, and root-mean-square error (RMSE) against the standard
deviation of the training target for continuous ones. A concept that fails to beat its baseline is reported as such rather
than aggregated away.

\subsection{Stage 2: symbolic decision head}
\label{sec:stage2}

The symbolic head consumes \emph{predicted} concepts $\hat c$, never ground
truth. This is the property that makes the pipeline interpretable end to end:
at inference no privileged information exists anywhere between pixels and
label, so the rule set is a complete description of the deployed decision.

For each Hubble class $k$ we fit one symbolic regression against the binary
one-vs-rest indicator $z = \mathbb{1}[y = k]$, following the formulation used
for symbolic star--galaxy--quasar
separation~\citep{deshpande2026}. Symbolic regression searches the space of
closed-form algebraic expressions directly: it returns a formula, not a set of
weights for a fixed functional form. The result is seven scalar functions
$g_k(\hat c)$ of the concept variables, and the predicted type is
$\arg\max_k g_k$. We use PySR~\citep{cranmer2023} with 100 iterations, a
maximum expression size of 25 nodes, parsimony $3.2\times10^{-3}$, binary
operators $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$ and unary operators
$\{\exp,\log,\sqrt{\cdot},(\cdot)^2,|\cdot|\}$, with argument-complexity
constraints on $\log$ and $\sqrt{\cdot}$ to suppress ill-conditioned
subexpressions, in deterministic serial mode.

We measure the size of an expression by its \emph{complexity}: the number of
nodes in its expression tree, counting each operator, each concept variable
and each fitted constant once. A complexity of $19$ therefore means nineteen
such elements, and the total over the seven classes is the number of symbols a
reader must hold in mind to reproduce the classifier by hand. The seven
adopted expressions are given in full in Appendix~\ref{app:rules}; they are
the deployed model, not a summary of it.

Symbolic regression returns a Pareto front trading training loss against
complexity; selecting by training loss overfits, and selecting by PySR's
internal score conflates the two objectives. We instead re-score every
frontier point by five-fold stratified cross-validation on held-out folds of
the training pool and select the expression with the highest cross-validated
accuracy, breaking ties toward lower complexity. Predicted class is the
$\arg\max$ over the seven per-class scores.

Rules are exported as \LaTeX, as plain text, and as an importable Python
module which Stage 3 and the cross-survey evaluation both consume directly.
The exported module \emph{is} the classifier; no separate serialised model
exists.

\subsection{Stage 3: conformal calibration}
\label{sec:stage3}

\paragraph{What this stage is for.}
A classifier that returns a single Hubble type gives the user no way to tell a
confident assignment from a guess. The usual substitute --- the network's own
softmax output --- is not a probability in any operational sense: it is a
monotone transformation of a score, and it is routinely overconfident. For a
morphology catalogue this matters concretely. If one selects Sd candidates for
follow-up, one needs to know how often the true type is actually among the
types the model was prepared to accept, and one needs that number to hold
without believing anything about the model's internals.

Conformal prediction supplies exactly that. Instead of a single label it
returns a \emph{set} of labels, sized so that the set contains the true type a
specified fraction of the time. The construction is purely empirical: it
measures on held-out data how wrong the model's scores typically are, and
inflates the set until the observed error rate matches the requested one. The
guarantee it delivers is finite-sample and distribution-free --- it holds for
any scoring function whatever, including a set of arbitrary analytic
expressions with no probabilistic interpretation, which is precisely our
situation. The price is that the answer is a set rather than a label, and the
average set size becomes the measure of how much the model actually knows: a
set of size one is a confident assignment, a set of size four is close to an
abstention.

\paragraph{Construction.}
Let $\pi_k(x) = \mathrm{softmax}_k[g(\hat c(x))]$ denote the normalised
symbolic scores. Using the least-ambiguous-set (LAC) conformity score
$s(x,y) = 1 - \pi_y(x)$ on the calibration split, we take $\hat q$ as the
$\lceil (n_{\rm cal}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil / n_{\rm cal}$ empirical quantile of
calibration scores and predict the set
$C(x) = \{k : 1 - \pi_k(x) \le \hat q\}$. Under exchangeability of calibration
and test data this satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,y \in C(x)\,\right] \;\ge\; 1-\alpha ,
\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
with no requirement that $\pi$ be a calibrated probability---which matters
here, since $\pi$ is a softmax over arbitrary analytic expressions and carries
no probabilistic semantics. We set $\alpha = 0.10$ and use
MAPIE~\citep{taquet2022}.

In words, and reading the symbols in turn: $x$ is a galaxy image and $y$ its
true Hubble type; $\hat c(x)$ are the concepts Stage~1 predicts from it;
$\pi_k(x)$ is the model's normalised preference for type $k$; the conformity
score $s(x,y) = 1 - \pi_y(x)$ is large when the model disliked the type that
turned out to be correct, so it measures how surprised the model was; $\hat q$
is the level of surprise that only the worst $10\%$ of calibration galaxies
exceeded; and the prediction set $C(x)$ collects every type the model finds at
least that plausible. The miscoverage level $\alpha = 0.10$ is the fraction of
galaxies we are willing to see fall outside their own prediction set;
$n_{\rm cal} = 4049$ is the number of calibration galaxies, and the ceiling in
$\hat q$ is the finite-sample correction that makes
Equation~\eqref{eq:coverage} exact rather than asymptotic.

\paragraph{Marginal is not conditional.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:coverage} is a statement about the marginal distribution:
it constrains the coverage averaged over all galaxies. It does \emph{not}
imply $\mathbb{P}[y \in C(x)\,|\,y=k] \ge 1-\alpha$ for each class $k$ --- that
the guarantee also holds separately within each morphological type. On a
sample where two thirds of the galaxies are ellipticals, a wrapper can satisfy
the first while failing the second badly, simply by covering ellipticals more
often than required. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} shows that this is what
happens here, and Section~\ref{sec:mondrian} implements the corresponding
class-conditional calibration and reports what it costs.

\subsection{Rule stability under survey shift}
\label{sec:stability}

\paragraph{What ``survey shift'' means physically.}
Two surveys observing the same galaxy do not record the same image. They
differ in angular resolution, so structure resolved by one is smoothed away by
the other; in passband, so each is sensitive to a different stellar
population, and a feature traced by young blue stars may simply be absent at
redder wavelengths; in depth, so low-surface-brightness features such as tidal
tails appear or vanish; and in point-spread function, which redistributes
light between the centre and the outskirts of a profile. Here the reference
survey is DECaLS at a $0.262''$ pixel scale, and the shifted survey is Euclid
at roughly $0.1''$ in a redder band. Non-parametric morphology statistics are
known to respond to all four differences~\citep{ferrari2015}, so both the
concept layer and any rule built on it are at risk under the change of
instrument, and the question is whether that risk is realised.

\paragraph{What is compared.}
Applying a trained model to a new survey tests only whether its \emph{answers}
survive. To test whether its \emph{reasoning} survives we need a second object
to compare against, so we perform a \emph{symbolic refit}: the entire Stage-2
procedure --- the same PySR settings, the same complexity budget, the same
cross-validated selection --- is run again from scratch on the shifted survey,
using that survey's own predicted concepts and labels. This yields a second,
independently discovered rule set for the same classification task. If the
task is instrument-independent, the two rule sets should invoke the same
physical quantities; if the reference rules encode something specific to
DECaLS imaging, they should not.

\paragraph{The comparison metric.}
For class $k$ let $U_k^{\rm ref}$ be the set of concept variables appearing in
the reference expression $g_k$ --- read off directly as the symbols the
formula contains --- and let $U_k^{\rm shift}$ be the corresponding set for
the Euclid refit. We compare these two sets rather than the two formulae
because algebraically different expressions can encode the same physical
reasoning: $v_{\rm ft}\,(1-v_{\rm M3})$ and $v_{\rm ft} - v_{\rm ft}v_{\rm M3}$
are different strings and the same statement. Concept usage, by contrast, is
the property an astronomer actually reads off a rule --- which measured
quantities the classification depends on --- and it is invariant to algebraic
rearrangement. We therefore quantify agreement with the Jaccard index, the
standard set-overlap measure, which asks what fraction of the concepts
mentioned by either rule set is mentioned by both. Its two limits are
interpretable without reference to any model: $J=1$ means the shifted survey
elicits reasoning over exactly the same physical quantities, and $J=0$ means
the two surveys yield disjoint explanations of the same morphological task. We
report the mean per-class Jaccard index
\begin{equation}
J = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \frac{|U_k^{\rm ref} \cap U_k^{\rm shift}|}
         {|U_k^{\rm ref} \cup U_k^{\rm shift}|},
\label{eq:jaccard}
\end{equation}
together with the Jaccard index of the five globally most-used concept
variables, where each variable is weighted by the cross-validated accuracy of
the rules invoking it so that the ranking is not dominated by a term appearing
once in a weak rule. The per-class index measures whether each individual
rule is rebuilt from the same ingredients; the top-five index measures whether
the pipeline as a whole still rests on the same handful of physical
quantities. A method whose explanations transfer should score high on both.

Equation~\eqref{eq:jaccard} is deliberately coarse: it registers \emph{which}
concepts a rule uses, not how strongly or in what direction it uses them. That
makes it a conservative test. Two rule sets can share every concept and still
combine them differently, so a high $J$ would not by itself prove that the
explanations agree --- but a low $J$ is unambiguous, since rules built from
different physical quantities cannot be the same explanation under any
reading.

statistics, $r_{\rm eff}$, $M_{20}$, Sérsic index and concentration are
predicted at roughly half the target dispersion or better.

Two concepts do not clear the bar, for different reasons. Smoothness is
genuinely not predicted better than its own standard deviation (RMSE $0.087$

residuals after smoothing, and their sensitivity to resolution and noise is
documented independently~\citep{ferrari2015}; their weakness here is
consistent with that literature rather than with a defect in the concept
predictor. All three are inputs available to the symbolic head, which is free
to ignore them---and, as Table~\ref{tab:rules} shows, the adopted rules do not
lean on smoothness or asymmetry for any class except Irr.

\subsection{The rule set}
\label{sec:rules}

The complete Stage-2 model is seven expressions of 17--25 nodes, 153 in total,
printed in full as Equations~\eqref{eq:rule_E}--\eqref{eq:rule_Irr} in
Appendix~\ref{app:rules}. Table~\ref{tab:rules} summarises
them, but the appendix is the model: everything asserted in this section can
be checked against it by hand, which is the point of building the classifier
this way. Of the thirty-nine concept variables available, the adopted rules
reference twenty.

\paragraph{Reading two of the rules.}
The elliptical score
\begin{equation*}
g_{\rm E} = v_{\rm sm}\left[\,1.004 - v_{\rm M3}
  + 0.690\,v_{\rm ft}\left(2S + v_{\rm cig} - v_{\rm ft} + 0.521\right)\right]
\end{equation*}
is the smooth-vote fraction $v_{\rm sm}$ multiplied by a bracket close to
unity, reduced when the merger vote $v_{\rm M3}$ is high. A galaxy is called
elliptical when volunteers see no structure and no interaction; the remaining
terms are second-order corrections carrying the cigar-shaped vote and the
measured clumpiness, both of which distinguish a genuinely smooth spheroid
from a smooth-looking edge-on disc. The Sd score
\begin{equation*}
g_{\rm Sd} = \sqrt{v_{\rm B0}}\;v_{\rm W_{l}}\,
  \left[v_{\rm ft}\,v_{\overline{\rm edge}}
  \left(v_{\rm M3} + v_{\overline{\rm arm}} - v_{\rm B2} - 1.175\right)\right]^{2}
\end{equation*}
is a product of four requirements, each of which must be satisfied for the
score to be appreciable: no bulge, loosely wound arms, a featured face-on
disc. That is the textbook definition of a late-type spiral, written in
volunteer votes. Neither expression needed to take this form, and neither was
constrained to.

\paragraph{Does the rule set recover the Galaxy Zoo branching structure?}
A claim about what an opaque search procedure discovered needs a check
external to the fitting itself, so we test this directly rather than asserting
it. Table~\ref{tab:treemap} reports,
for each class rule, which nodes of the Galaxy Zoo decision tree the rule
draws concepts from; the entries are read mechanically off the free symbols of
the expressions in Appendix~\ref{app:rules}.

The correspondence is close but not exact, and both halves matter. The
spiral-geometry nodes---edge-on, bulge size and arm winding---are used by
exactly the four spiral classes Sa--Sd and by no other class. In the Galaxy
Zoo tree these questions are asked only after a galaxy has been called
featured, non-edge-on and arm-bearing, so the rules have recovered the tree's
spiral sub-branch and confined it to the classes it governs. Roundedness,
which the tree asks only on the \emph{smooth} branch, appears in E, S0 and Irr
and in none of the spirals. The merger node is used by all seven rules,
consistent with its position as a question asked of every galaxy.

Two departures should be stated plainly. First, six of the seven rules factor
through the root task, smooth-or-featured; the irregular rule does not, keying
instead on the merger node and the measured clumpiness $S$. The correspondence
is therefore not universal, and the exception is defensible on its own terms:
disturbance, not absence of structure, is what defines the irregular class. Second, the bar and
arm-count nodes appear only in the irregular rule, where they enter an
exponential factor alongside roundedness. These are disc questions appearing
outside the disc branch, and we do not claim a physical mechanism for it; the
likely reading is that ``strong bar'' and ``more than four arms'' votes are
elevated for chaotic systems where volunteers see structure they cannot
resolve, making them incidental markers of disturbance rather than of bars or
arms. We flag it as the one place where the recovered usage does not track the
tree.

The recovery is therefore substantial and partial: the tree's branching order
is reproduced where the classes are well populated, and it breaks down for the
class defined by disturbance.

\paragraph{What the printed equations expose.}
Two properties are visible in Appendix~\ref{app:rules} that no summary would
have shown. The Sa rule carries a pole: its denominator $0.636 - v_{\rm B0}$
vanishes when the no-bulge vote fraction reaches $0.636$, so the score
diverges on a thin set of inputs. On this sample the pole is never approached
closely enough to affect a prediction, but it is a latent instability that
would matter on a survey with a different bulge-vote distribution, and it is
grounds for refitting the Sa rule with a bounded operator set before deploying
it elsewhere. The Sb rule, by contrast, is a ratio with a strictly positive
denominator and is bounded everywhere on $[0,1]^{39}$. A dense linear head
would have concealed the first and offered no way to establish the second.
This is the concrete sense in which the rule table buys something: a reader
can audit the functional form for pathologies, not merely inspect a ranking of
weights.

\paragraph{Are the rules explanatory, or merely short?}
Compactness and legibility are not the same property, and an expression can be
sparse without being interpretable. We therefore state which of the seven
rules we think are genuinely explanatory and which are not, so the reader can
disagree with the assessment against the printed equations rather than take it
on trust.

Four read directly as morphological statements. Equation~\eqref{eq:rule_E} is
the smooth vote suppressed by merger evidence; \eqref{eq:rule_S0} is a featured
non-merging system with no arms and an elongated profile;
\eqref{eq:rule_Sb} is a face-on featured disc with tightly wound arms, damped
when a large bulge, an absence of arms or a merger signature is present; and
\eqref{eq:rule_Sd} is the four-way product described above. In each case the
sign and role of every term can be stated in a sentence.

Three are less satisfactory. Equation~\eqref{eq:rule_Sa} has an interpretable
numerator---large bulge, tight winding, face-on, featured---but divides it by
$0.636 - v_{\rm B0}$, and we can offer no morphological reading of that
denominator beyond its role as a steep monotone gain near the pole.
Equation~\eqref{eq:rule_Sc} is a product of two squared factors, and squaring
discards the sign of each, so the expression is legible term by term but its
behaviour as a whole is not obvious by inspection. Equation~\eqref{eq:rule_Irr}
is the weakest on this criterion: it is compact, at $25$ nodes, but it is the
modulus of a product of two shifted exponential terms, and while one can see
that it responds to merger votes and to clumpiness, its functional shape is not
one a morphologist would have written down.

We take this to be an honest limit on the interpretability claim rather than a
refutation of it. Symbolic regression optimises for accuracy at a complexity
budget, not for explanatory form, and nothing in the search rewards a rule for
being a recognisable morphological statement. That four of seven nonetheless
are is evidence that the concept layer carries genuine physical structure; that
three are not is a reason to treat ``symbolic'' and ``interpretable'' as
distinct properties, and to report which rules meet the stronger standard
rather than claiming it for the set as a whole. Constraining the operator set,
or penalising nested transcendental subexpressions, would push the search
toward explanatory form and is the obvious next step.

\paragraph{Is the adopted expression size justified?}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the PySR Pareto frontier per class together with
the adopted expression. Every frontier rises steeply below roughly ten nodes
and is close to flat thereafter, which invites the objection that the adopted
17--25 node expressions are avoidable complexity, or that the
cross-validated selection has mildly overfitted the frontier. We test this
directly. From each frontier we take the \emph{smallest} expression whose
cross-validated accuracy is within $0.005$ of the class best, assemble those
seven into an alternative rule set, and evaluate it end to end on the same
splits. The comparison is reported in Table~\ref{tab:compact}.

On the aggregate metrics the objection is largely upheld. The compact set uses
$31$ nodes against $153$, a reduction of $80\%$, and on the test split it is
indistinguishable from the adopted set: accuracy $0.776$ against $0.776$ and
$\kappa$ equal to four decimal places ($\Delta\kappa = -0.00007$). On
validation, the split on which the adopted rules were selected, it is behind by
$0.007$ in accuracy and $0.012$ in $\kappa$. That the gap is visible on
validation and absent on test is itself the signature of mild selection
overfitting. A practitioner who cares about
accuracy or $\kappa$ should therefore take the $31$-node set: four nodes for
E, seven for S0, eight for Sb, nine for Sc, and a single node each for Sa, Sd
and Irr.

The macro-$F_1$ column tells a different story. Macro-$F_1$ falls from $0.462$
to $0.421$ on validation, and inspection of the compact rules explains why. The
cross-validated one-vs-rest criterion selects a bare \emph{constant} for both
Sa ($0.00203$) and Sd ($0.00428$). A constant is a legitimate point on the
frontier and scores $0.998$ one-vs-rest accuracy on a class present in $0.2\%$
of objects, simply by never predicting it---but a rule that never fires removes
its class from the model's output entirely. The compact classifier predicts Sa
and Sd for no galaxy in either split.

The gap is almost entirely one class, and it is worth being precise about how
small it is. The adopted rules recover Sd at $F_1 = 0.222$ on validation and
$0.118$ on test, correctly identifying $4$ and $2$ of $26$ objects
respectively. Sa is recovered by \emph{neither} rule set: the adopted
classifier predicts it once or twice per split and is wrong every time, giving
$F_1 = 0$ on both splits, exactly as Table~\ref{tab:balance} and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} would lead one to expect from $49$ training
examples. So the honest accounting is that the additional $122$ nodes buy a
handful of correctly classified Sd galaxies and nothing else measurable.

This is a weakness in our own Stage-2 selection statistic that the question
exposed, and we report it as such. One-vs-rest cross-validated accuracy is
close to degenerate for a class at $0.2\%$ prevalence, because the constant
predictor is nearly optimal under it; the adopted rules escape the trap only
because the full-complexity search happened to find expressions scoring
marginally higher. A class-balanced selection statistic---balanced accuracy or
one-vs-rest $F_1$---would be the principled correction, and we recommend it for
any application of this architecture to an imbalanced sample. We have not
adopted it here because doing so requires refitting Stage 2 and would change
every number in the paper.

The conclusion we draw is narrower than a simple complexity-budget argument
would suggest, and we state the trade rather than settle it. The $25$-node cap
of
Section~\ref{sec:stage2} is not what limits performance, and the accuracy
available to a symbolic head over this concept vocabulary is essentially
exhausted by a handful of terms per class. A reader whose priority is legibility
should take the $31$-node set, and we would not argue against that choice: it
costs nothing in accuracy or $\kappa$ and it is five times smaller. We retain
the longer expressions because a classifier that silently drops a Hubble type is
a different object from one that predicts it badly, and because the compact
set's route to parity---deleting the classes it cannot model---is the behaviour
this paper is written to make visible. Both rule sets are released so the choice
need not be taken on our authority.

\subsection{Comparison against baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the symbolic head against three
alternatives trained on identical splits: a dense linear CBM head over the
same predicted concepts, gradient-boosted trees over the same concepts, and an
end-to-end ConvNeXt classifier with no bottleneck.

The unconstrained network is the most accurate model. It reaches accuracy
$0.802$ and $\kappa = 0.601$, ahead of the symbolic head by $0.015$ and
$0.030$ respectively. We report this plainly: passing predictions through a
seventeen-concept bottleneck and then through an analytic rule costs
approximately three points of $\kappa$ on this sample. Interpretability is not
free here, and any claim that it is would not survive the comparison.

The three concept-based models---symbolic, dense linear, boosted trees---are
mutually indistinguishable, spanning $0.001$ in accuracy and $0.002$ in
$\kappa$. The bottleneck, not the choice of head, is what separates them from
the network. This localises the cost: it is the restriction to seventeen named
concepts that gives up the three points, not the decision to express the final
classifier symbolically rather than densely. A practitioner who wants the
concept layer for auditability therefore pays once, and can then choose the
symbolic head at no further accuracy cost but with a large gain in legibility.

Macro-$F_1$ orders the models differently from accuracy and $\kappa$. The
symbolic head attains $0.462$ against the network's $0.448$, and the three
concept-based models all exceed the network on this metric. Accuracy and
$\kappa$ are dominated by the elliptical class, which supplies two thirds of
the sample; macro-$F_1$ weights all seven classes equally and therefore
reflects performance on the rare morphologies. The gap is small---$0.014$,
with Sa and Sd contributing at $n=12$ and $n=26$---and we do not claim it as a
significant advantage. We note only that the accuracy ranking and the
class-balanced ranking disagree, and that a reader interested in rare
morphologies should not select a model on accuracy alone.

The interpretability cost column makes the remaining trade explicit. The
symbolic head carries 153 operator nodes and is printed in full in
Table~\ref{tab:rules}; the network carries $1.5\times10^{7}$ trainable
parameters and cannot be printed at all. Three points of $\kappa$ is the price
of that difference on this dataset.

\subsection{Do interpretable models agree with each other?}
\label{sec:agreement}

A concept-importance ranking is only useful if it is stable across reasonable
modelling choices. We compare the symbolic head's concept usage---each concept
weighted by the cross-validated accuracy of the rules invoking it---against the
linear head's coefficient magnitudes and against gradient-boosted-tree feature
importances, over the twenty concept variables the symbolic rule set actually
references (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}), which are the variables on which all
three models can be compared.

Agreement is moderate: Spearman $\rho = 0.51$ (Pearson $0.60$) against the
linear head, and $\rho = 0.42$ (Pearson $0.67$) against boosted trees
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fidelity}). Three models trained on identical inputs, all
reaching the same accuracy, therefore rank the physical drivers of morphology
only partially consistently. This bounds how strongly any single model's
concept ranking should be read as a physical statement, and it argues for
reporting concept importance from an intrinsically interpretable model whose
ranking is the decision rule rather than a summary of one.

\subsection{Calibration: marginal validity, conditional failure}
\label{sec:calibration}

At $\alpha = 0.10$ the conformal wrapper attains $88.9\%$ empirical coverage
on the test split against a $90\%$ target, a deviation of $-0.011$, well
inside the finite-sample tolerance of $0.038$. Mean prediction-set size is
$1.46$ of seven classes and $66\%$ of test objects receive a singleton set.
Equation~\eqref{eq:coverage} is satisfied.

Table~\ref{tab:coverage} shows what that guarantee conceals. Coverage
conditional on true class ranges from $0.982$ for ellipticals to $0.083$ for
Sa. Irregulars are covered $45\%$ of the time and Sd $46\%$, against a nominal
$90\%$. The marginal guarantee is met because ellipticals constitute two
thirds of the sample and are over-covered; every rare class is far below
nominal.

This behaviour is expected---marginal conformal prediction makes no
conditional promise---but its magnitude here has direct scientific
consequence. A researcher selecting irregular or Sd candidates from this
catalogue and trusting the nominal $90\%$ would be operating at roughly half
that coverage, and for Sa the prediction sets are essentially uninformative.
Reporting only marginal coverage in a morphology context is therefore
misleading, and we recommend that per-class coverage accompany any conformal
result on imbalanced astronomical samples.

The sets also support a selective-prediction workflow.
Figure~\ref{fig:selective} plots accuracy on the retained sample against the
fraction abstained, under two acceptance rules: ranking by softmax confidence,
and accepting only when the conformal set contains fewer than $k$ classes.
Retained accuracy rises from $0.776$ on the full test split to $0.868$ at a
quarter deferred and $0.926$ at half, after which it is flat. A user willing
to send ambiguous objects to visual inspection can therefore trade
completeness for purity in a quantified way, and the plateau beyond $50\%$
abstention indicates that the residual errors are not ones the model's own
confidence can identify.

\subsection{Repairing conditional coverage, and what it costs}
\label{sec:mondrian}

Identifying a reliability failure is of limited use without establishing
whether it can be fixed, so we implement the fix rather than deferring it. The
standard remedy is class-conditional, or Mondrian, conformal
prediction~\citep{vovk2005,ding2023classcond}: instead of one threshold
calibrated on the pooled calibration set, one threshold $\hat q_k$ is
calibrated per class, using only the calibration galaxies whose true type is
$k$,
\begin{equation}
C(x) = \left\{k : 1 - \pi_k(x) \le \hat q_k\right\},\qquad
\hat q_k = \mathrm{Quantile}\!\left(\{s_i\}_{y_i = k};\,
  \frac{\lceil (n_k+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n_k}\right),
\label{eq:mondrian}
\end{equation}
which delivers the conditional guarantee
$\mathbb{P}[y \in C(x)\,|\,y=k] \ge 1-\alpha$ for every class separately.
Nothing upstream is retrained: Stage 1 and Stage 2 are frozen and only the
calibration step changes, so the comparison isolates the effect of the
calibration scheme.

Equation~\eqref{eq:mondrian} carries a hard requirement that the marginal
version does not. The quantile level exceeds unity, and hence no finite
threshold is valid, unless
$n_k \ge \lceil 1/\alpha \rceil - 1$, which at $\alpha = 0.10$ means at least
nine calibration galaxies of that class. Our calibration split contains eight
Sa galaxies (Table~\ref{tab:balance}). For Sa, therefore, no threshold
computed from these data can certify $90\%$ conditional coverage, and the only
valid choice is $\hat q_{\rm Sa} = \infty$: the class enters every prediction
set. This is not a defect of the method but the honest content of the data ---
eight galaxies cannot underwrite a $90\%$ guarantee --- and it is worth
stating as a design rule, since it converts an abstract sample-size concern
into a concrete threshold that a survey team can check before calibrating.

Table~\ref{tab:mondrian} and Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} report the outcome, and
it is a trade rather than an improvement.

Conditional validity is restored. Every class with a sufficient calibration
count is brought to nominal: coverage runs from $0.885$ for Sd to $0.917$ for
Sc, against a $0.90$ target, and the worst per-class coverage rises from
$0.083$ to $0.885$. The over-coverage of the dominant class disappears as it
should---ellipticals fall from $0.982$ to $0.894$---confirming that the
marginal guarantee had been financed by them. Marginal coverage is unaffected
($0.889 \rightarrow 0.896$), so nothing is lost on the statistic the submitted
version reported.

The price is severe. Mean prediction-set size rises from $1.46$ to $3.94$ of
seven classes, and the fraction of galaxies receiving a singleton set falls
from $0.659$ to \emph{zero}. Under class-conditional calibration no galaxy in
the test split is assigned a unique morphological type; the median set contains
three. The mechanism is visible in the fitted thresholds: because a rare class
has few calibration objects and the model scores it weakly, its threshold
$\hat q_k$ sits near $0.88$, which admits that class to almost every set. The
guarantee is met by making the rare classes nearly always available rather than
by making the model better at recognising them.

That distinction is the substantive result. Class-conditional calibration
repairs the \emph{statement} the catalogue makes about its own reliability
without repairing the \emph{information} it carries, and on this sample the
repair costs the catalogue its per-object usefulness. A user who needs a
$90\%$ guarantee for irregulars can have it, but the accompanying answer is
``this galaxy is one of these four types''. The binding constraint is therefore
not the calibration scheme but the class imbalance and the calibration budget
underneath it: with $2701$ elliptical and $8$ Sa calibration galaxies, no
post-hoc procedure can deliver both conditional validity and informative sets.
We report both schemes for this reason, and recommend that any morphology
pipeline adopting conformal prediction report set size and singleton fraction
alongside coverage, since a coverage figure alone conceals which of the two
regimes it is operating in.

\subsection{Cross-survey transfer}
\label{sec:robustness}

We apply the frozen Stage-1$\rightarrow$2$\rightarrow$3 pipeline to
$161\,395$ Euclid Q1 galaxies and, separately, refit the symbolic head on a
stratified Euclid subsample to measure rule stability
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}, Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}).

Classification transfers. Accuracy rises by $0.052$ and $\kappa$ by $0.170$
relative to the DECaLS reference. This is not a majority-class artefact:
Euclid's label distribution is \emph{less} elliptical-dominated ($57.2\%$
versus $66.8\%$), which should if anything depress a prior-driven classifier.
We caution that the comparison is not controlled---the two surveys differ in
depth, resolution, passband and volunteer campaign, and the Euclid labels come
from a different vote-fraction distribution---so we report the transfer as an
observation, not as evidence that Euclid morphology is intrinsically easier.

Calibration degrades. The expected calibration error (ECE), the average gap
between a model's confidence and its accuracy, rises by $0.083$: the
pipeline becomes more accurate and simultaneously less able to signal when it
should not be trusted. Empirical coverage also rises, by $0.074$ to $0.963$
against the same $0.90$ target. Over-coverage is not a violation of
Equation~\eqref{eq:coverage}---the guarantee is one-sided---but it is a
symptom of the same problem: the threshold $\hat q$ calibrated on DECaLS is
too conservative for Euclid, so prediction sets are systematically larger than
they need to be and carry less information per object. Exchangeability between
calibration and test data, which the conformal guarantee assumes, does not
survive the change of instrument. Confidence ranking is the first casualty of
domain shift, before accuracy.

Explanations do not transfer. A symbolic refit on Euclid recovers a mean
per-class concept-usage Jaccard of $0.420$ and a top-five concept overlap of
$0.429$. Fewer than half the concepts that the reference rules rely on
reappear when the same procedure is run on the shifted survey. The functional
form of ``how to recognise a spiral'' is instrument-dependent even where the
resulting labels are not.

This is the paper's central methodological point. An accuracy-only robustness
audit of this pipeline would report improvement under shift and certify it as
transferable. Two additional measurements---conditional coverage and rule
stability---show that the uncertainty quantification and the explanation have
both silently changed. Accuracy transfer, calibration transfer and
interpretation transfer are three separate properties, and only the first is
routinely reported.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the rule table buys}

The deployed classifier is Equations~\eqref{eq:rule_E}--\eqref{eq:rule_Irr}.
A reader can check that the elliptical rule keys on the smooth vote and the
absence of merger signatures, that the spiral subtypes separate on winding and
bulge size, and that the Sd rule is a product of four independent
requirements; and can identify exactly which concept would have to change, and
by how much, to flip any given classification. That audit is available for a
black-box network only through a surrogate whose faithfulness is itself in
question~\citep{rudin2019}, and Section~\ref{sec:agreement} shows that
surrogate rankings disagree substantially with the intrinsic rule even when
accuracy matches.

The audit is not merely a restatement of the accuracy result. It surfaced the
pole in the Sa rule (Section~\ref{sec:rules}), a latent numerical instability
that no aggregate metric reports and that a dense head would have hidden
inside a weight; it establishes that nineteen of the thirty-nine available
concept variables---including every structural statistic except
smoothness---are not used at all, which is a substantive negative result about
what non-parametric morphology contributes once perceptual concepts are
available; and it permits the branching-structure test of
Table~\ref{tab:treemap}, which found the correspondence with the Galaxy Zoo
tree to be close for the well-populated classes and to break down for the
irregular class. Each of these is a criticism a reader can now level at the
model. That is what we mean by the claim that interpretability is delivered
rather than asserted: the model is exposed to objections that a black box
would not have permitted.

What that audit costs is now quantified rather than asserted: $0.030$ in
$\kappa$ against an unconstrained network (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).
Because the three concept-based models agree to within $0.002$, the price is
attributable to the concept layer and not to the symbolic form of the head.
This matters for anyone adopting the architecture. Having accepted a concept
bottleneck for auditability, one may take the symbolic head---and with it a
classifier small enough to print---without giving up anything further. The
design decision worth deliberating is whether to impose the bottleneck at all;
the choice of head, once inside it, is close to free.

\subsection{What conditional coverage means for rare-object science}

The morphologies of greatest astrophysical interest are frequently the rarest:
mergers, disturbed systems, edge-on discs with dust lanes. Table~\ref{tab:coverage}
shows these are exactly the classes for which marginal conformal guarantees
fail hardest. Any selection function built on nominal coverage will be
optimistic by a factor of two for irregulars in this sample. We regard this as
the most practically consequential result here.

Section~\ref{sec:mondrian} implements the corresponding fix rather than
recommending it in the abstract, and the outcome revises what we would
recommend. Class-conditional calibration does restore per-class validity:
worst-case coverage rises from $0.083$ to $0.885$, and the over-covering of
ellipticals that had been financing the marginal guarantee disappears. But the
guarantee is bought, not conjured. Mean set size roughly triples, from $1.46$
to $3.94$ of seven classes, and the singleton fraction falls from $0.659$ to
zero: under conditional calibration no galaxy in the test split receives a
unique morphological type.

The reason is worth separating from the arithmetic. The conditional threshold
for a rare class sits near $0.88$ because that class has few calibration
objects and is scored weakly by the model, so it is admitted to almost every
prediction set. Validity is therefore achieved by keeping the rare classes
perpetually available rather than by improving discrimination. The reliability
\emph{statement} is repaired; the \emph{information} is not. On a sample with
$2701$ elliptical and $8$ Sa calibration galaxies, no post-hoc calibration can
deliver conditional validity and informative sets at once, because the
shortfall is in the data rather than in the wrapper.

Two practical consequences follow. First, conditional validity at miscoverage
$\alpha$ requires at least $\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil - 1$ calibration objects
\emph{per class}---nine at $90\%$, nineteen at $95\%$. That should govern how a
calibration split is allocated, stratified toward the rare classes rather than
proportionally, and should be checked before calibrating rather than
discovered afterwards in a coverage table. Second, coverage should never be
reported alone. A pipeline quoting $0.90$ coverage may be returning singletons
or may be returning half the label space, and only set size and singleton
fraction distinguish the two. We recommend reporting per-class coverage, mean
set size and singleton fraction together, and we do not recommend
class-conditional calibration unconditionally: it is the right choice when a
per-class guarantee is what the science needs, and the wrong one when a usable
per-object label is.

\subsection{Interpretation transfer as an evaluation target}

Domain-shift studies in astronomy report accuracy degradation and remedy it by
retraining or domain adaptation. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} shows that the
explanation can change even when accuracy does not, which no accuracy-based
diagnostic detects. If interpretable models are to be deployed across the
survey landscape of the next decade---Euclid, LSST, Roman---then rule
stability under instrument change is a property that must be measured, not
assumed. Equation~\eqref{eq:jaccard} is a deliberately simple instrument for
this; more refined measures of functional-form equivalence are an open
problem.

The physical driver of the instability is plausibly the resolution and
passband dependence of the concept layer itself~\citep{ferrari2015}: if
smoothness and asymmetry mean different things at $0.262''$ and $0.1''$
sampling, rules built on them cannot be expected to survive. Disentangling
concept-level from rule-level instability requires matched-resolution
experiments we have not performed.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We state the constraints on these results explicitly.

\emph{Class imbalance dominates the aggregate metrics.} With 49 Sa and 104 Sd
training examples, no conclusion about these classes is supported by these
data. Macro-$F_1$ of $0.462$ against accuracy of $0.787$ is a direct
consequence, and the high one-vs-rest CV accuracies in
Table~\ref{tab:rules} for rare classes reflect class prior, not discrimination.

\emph{Interpretability costs accuracy here.} The unconstrained network leads
the symbolic head by $0.030$ in $\kappa$ (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). We do not
claim that symbolic regression is the better classifier. The claim is narrower:
the concept bottleneck carries a measurable and modest price, the symbolic form
adds nothing to that price, and the resulting model is auditable. Whether three
points of $\kappa$ is worth paying is an application-dependent judgement we
leave to the reader.

\emph{Baseline training budget.} All models are evaluated at their best
validation checkpoint. The network overfits within a few epochs at this sample
size and its early-stopped optimum may not be its ceiling; a larger training
set (Section~\ref{sec:data}) would likely favour it further, which would widen
rather than close the gap we report.

\emph{The concept vocabulary is closed.} Structural properties absent from
both the Galaxy Zoo tree and \textsc{statmorph} cannot enter a rule, and
therefore cannot be diagnosed as a cause of transfer failure. Our inability to
attribute the Euclid rule instability to a specific instrumental cause follows
from this.

\emph{The cross-survey comparison is uncontrolled.} DECaLS and Euclid Q1
differ in depth, resolution, passband and volunteer campaign simultaneously.
The transfer numbers in Table~\ref{tab:robustness} describe what happens, not
why.

\emph{Two concepts fail.} Smoothness does not beat its trivial baseline and
asymmetry is marginal; rules invoking them inherit that weakness. This bears
directly on Equations~\eqref{eq:rule_E} and~\eqref{eq:rule_Irr}, the only two
that use a structural statistic, and both use smoothness. Their smoothness
terms should be read as weakly determined.

\emph{Class-conditional calibration is bounded by the calibration split.}
Section~\ref{sec:mondrian} restores per-class validity only for classes with
at least nine calibration galaxies. For Sa it degenerates to unconditional
inclusion, which is valid but uninformative, and across all classes it
eliminates singleton predictions entirely. A stratified calibration split
guaranteeing the minimum count per class would improve the first problem but
not the second, which is driven by the imbalance itself. We did not re-allocate
the splits here because doing so would have changed the sample underlying every
other result in the paper.

\emph{The Stage-2 selection statistic is degenerate for the rarest classes.}
The compact-rule ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rules}) showed that one-vs-rest
cross-validated accuracy selects a bare constant for Sa and Sd whenever a
constant is on the frontier, because a rule that never fires is right $99.8\%$
of the time on a class at $0.2\%$ prevalence. The adopted rules avoid this
outcome, but by a small margin rather than by construction. A class-balanced
selection statistic is the principled fix; applying it requires refitting
Stage 2 and would change every number reported here, so we identify the
problem rather than correct it.

\emph{The compact-rule ablation searches only the cached frontiers.} It asks
whether a shorter expression \emph{already discovered} by the search performs
comparably, not whether a different search configuration would find a better
short expression. A dedicated low-complexity run is a stronger test and was
not performed.

\emph{The symbolic refit is subsampled.} Euclid rule stability is computed on
a $3000$-object stratified subsample for tractability. The indices should be
reconfirmed at full survey scale.

\emph{Softmax scores are not probabilities.} Conformal coverage holds
regardless, but the ECE shift in Table~\ref{tab:robustness} should be read as
degraded confidence \emph{ranking}, not as miscalibration of a probabilistic
model.

\emph{One shifted survey.} The HST/JWST leg is pending data access; the
three-survey claim is currently a two-survey result.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We built a Concept Bottleneck Model for galaxy morphology in which a symbolic
rule set of 153 operator nodes performs the final classification, wrapped in
split-conformal prediction. The rule set is printed in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:rules}, so every claim below about how the model reasons can
be checked against the model itself. On $40\,498$ Galaxy Zoo Evo galaxies:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The concept bottleneck costs $0.030$ in $\kappa$ relative to an
unconstrained ConvNeXt, while the symbolic head, a dense linear head and
gradient-boosted trees over the same concepts agree to within $0.002$. The
price is paid for the bottleneck, not for the symbolic form, and buys a
classifier of 153 operator nodes in place of $1.5\times10^{7}$ parameters. On
macro-$F_1$ the ordering reverses and every concept-based model exceeds the
network.
\item Fifteen of seventeen concepts are recovered above trivial baselines. The
discovered rules reproduce the branching structure of the Galaxy Zoo decision
tree for the well-populated classes without supervision on that structure:
the spiral-geometry questions appear in exactly the four spiral rules and the
roundedness question in exactly the three non-spiral rules. The correspondence
fails for the irregular class, which does not use the root task at all.
\item Marginal conformal coverage is exact ($0.889$ against $0.90$) while
conditional coverage spans $0.98$ to $0.08$ across classes. Marginal
guarantees are inadequate for rare-morphology science, and per-class coverage
should be reported as standard. Class-conditional calibration restores
per-class validity---worst-case coverage $0.083 \rightarrow 0.885$---but
triples mean set size to $3.94$ of seven classes and eliminates singleton
predictions entirely. The reliability statement is repaired; the information is
not, because the shortfall is in the calibration budget rather than in the
wrapper. Conditional validity additionally requires at least
$\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil - 1$ calibration objects per class, which should govern
how calibration splits are allocated.
\item The adopted $153$-node rule set can be replaced by a $31$-node one with
no measurable loss in accuracy or $\kappa$ on test. What the extra nodes buy is
macro-$F_1$: the compact selection returns a constant for the two rarest
classes, which then disappear from the model's output entirely, whereas the
adopted set still recovers a handful of Sd galaxies. Neither set recovers Sa.
One-vs-rest cross-validated accuracy is a degenerate selection criterion at
$0.2\%$ prevalence, and class-balanced selection should be preferred.
\item Under DECaLS $\rightarrow$ Euclid transfer, accuracy is preserved while
calibration degrades and symbolic refits recover under half the reference
concept usage. Accuracy transfer does not imply interpretation transfer.
\end{enumerate}

The third and fourth findings generalise beyond this model. Any interpretable
classifier deployed across surveys inherits both failure modes, and neither is
visible to the accuracy-based diagnostics currently reported. We release the
benchmark so that concept fidelity, conditional coverage and rule stability
can be measured for any morphology pipeline.

\section{The deployed symbolic rule set}
\label{app:rules}

This appendix prints the Stage-2 classifier in full. These seven expressions
\emph{are} the deployed model: at inference the pipeline evaluates exactly
these formulae on the predicted concept variables and returns
$\arg\max_k g_k$. No weight matrix, serialised network or post-processing step
stands between what is printed here and what the classifier does. A reader
with Table~\ref{tab:concepts}, the symbol dictionary in
Table~\ref{tab:symbols} and a calculator can reproduce any prediction the
model makes.

Each $v$ denotes a \emph{predicted} vote fraction for one answer to one Galaxy
Zoo task, in $[0,1]$: $v_{\rm sm}$, for instance, is the fraction of
volunteers the Stage-1 network predicts would call the galaxy smooth. Bars
denote negative answers, so $v_{\overline{\rm arm}}$ is the predicted fraction
answering that no spiral arms are visible. $S$ is the \textsc{statmorph}
smoothness (clumpiness) statistic, the only structural concept any adopted
rule uses. Of the thirty-nine available concept variables the rules reference
twenty; the remaining nineteen, including concentration, Gini, $M_{20}$, the
S\'ersic index and the effective radius, are available to the search and were
not selected.
